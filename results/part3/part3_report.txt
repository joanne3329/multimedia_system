Part 3: Landing on the Pixel Grid

Part 3.I - area coverage:
The five-pointed star was rotated by 5 degrees so that no edge is exactly horizontal or vertical.
For each k, the path and canvas were scaled by k and filled using the Part 2 scanline algorithm.
Each k-by-k block was then averaged into one output pixel. No built-in anti-aliasing or smoothing function was used.

Part 3.II - measurement:
Selected pixel row: y = 216
Shallow edge endpoints: [188.2838837059387, 210.13184044746708] to [624.1051149006041, 248.26125750091106]
At k=1, the profile contains an abrupt binary step with values of only 0 or 1.

Measured growth when k doubles:
k=1 to k=2: 4.438 times
k=2 to k=4: 1.936 times
k=4 to k=8: 3.237 times
The enlarged image contains k^2 times as many sample positions. Therefore, doubling k produces four times as many high-resolution pixels.
The measured wall-clock time does not necessarily increase by exactly four times because NumPy processes row slices and block averaging using vector operations.

Part 3.III - where the grid loses:
The wheel contains 48 wedge subpaths, with gaps as wide as the wedges.
(a) At k=1, the center shows aliasing, including false and unstable radial patterns rather than only jagged edges.
(b) Moving toward the center, the wedges and gaps become narrower, so their spatial frequency increases.
Once the spatial frequency exceeds the Nyquist limit of the final pixel grid, the Sampling Theorem says that the detail cannot be represented or recovered.
Increasing k cleans the outer edges, but the center approaches uniform gray instead of recovering the individual wedges.
(c) A low-pass anti-aliasing filter is relevant here.
Averaging every k-by-k block already applies a box low-pass filter before downsampling.
It suppresses misleading high-frequency aliases and produces average area coverage, but it cannot recover details that the final pixel grid cannot represent.
